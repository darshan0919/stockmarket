## How to classify a company before picking a multiple

1. Identify the primary sector (use Screener.in's sector/industry tag as the starting point, but override if the business model doesn't match — e.g., a "chemicals" company that's actually a CDMO pharma-adjacent business should use the CDMO row, not generic chemicals).
2. Check if it's a conglomerate (multiple unrelated segments with different natural multiples) → use **SOTP**, not a single blended multiple (see §Conglomerates below).
3. Check if it's a holding company (majority stake in a listed subsidiary, little independent operating business) → use **look-through NAV + historical discount**, not P/E on the holdco's own thin standalone P&L.
4. Otherwise, look up the sector table below for the primary multiple and supporting KPIs.
5. If a sector isn't listed, default to: P/E for asset-light/low-depreciation businesses, EV/EBITDA for asset-heavy businesses with material depreciation, and always check margin trend over 8–10 years before trusting any point-in-time multiple (see "Over-earning check" below).

## Over-earning / cycle check (apply before trusting ANY multiple, any sector)

A cheap-looking multiple on peak, over-earning margins is not actually cheap. Before using a multiple:
- Pull 8–10 years of margin history. Is the current margin near a multi-year high or low?
- If near a high: check whether management itself has been walking guidance down over recent quarters (a repeated pattern — e.g. specialty chemical names guiding "32%→24-26%→21-22%" margin over successive quarters is the tell that the market hasn't yet re-priced for reversion).
- If near a low: check whether this is genuine structural impairment (competitive entry, regulatory, oversupply) vs a temporary cyclical trough that reverts (capital-cycle logic: oversupply → margin compression → capitulation/exits → supply rationalizes → margin recovers).
- Nifty long-run reference: aggregate earnings growth ~12–14% CAGR, aggregate ROE ~14% — useful anchor for "is this company/sector's current trajectory unusually good or bad vs the market's own long-run mean."

## Rate sensitivity (apply as a multiplier-adjustment overlay, all sectors)

Rising discount rates compress P/E most for high-growth/high-multiple names (terminal value ≈ 55-60% of total DCF value, so it's disproportionately rate-sensitive) and least for low-growth/value names. When comparing a current multiple to a course-era anchor, ask whether the risk-free-rate backdrop has shifted materially since — if 10Y yields have moved meaningfully, shift the acceptable multiple range in the same direction the anchor era's yields imply.

## General valuation techniques (cross-sector — apply alongside the sector table)

These are company-agnostic techniques and diagnostics, additive to the DCF/reverse-DCF/PEG/EV-EBITDA/Price-Sales frameworks already covered above. Apply them to any company regardless of sector, before or alongside picking the sector-specific primary metric.

1. **Quality-bucket migration framework.** Businesses migrate through valuation "buckets" as the underlying business quality changes — roughly: B5 (gruesome/cyclical trouble) → B4 (good business, low earnings growth) → B3 (good business, high earnings growth, large opportunity) → B2/B1 (great business). **Valuation follows business-quality change, not the reverse** — don't expect a re-rating without first identifying what changed operationally. Worked examples: PI Industries 23x→58x P/E as agrochem MNC-consolidation uncertainty resolved and CDMO order flow resumed; APL Apollo 10x→35x P/E tracking HRC steel price / EBITDA-per-tonne (a semi-commodity business — best bought when EBITDA/ton is cyclically depressed, not when it's rich); Garware Technical Fibres 5x→33x as value-added-product mix rose from 35%→70% of the portfolio; DVS Labs 35x→12x on a USFDA observation, then re-rated to ~50x once resolved within a record ~12 months — the *speed and quality of resolution* of a regulatory overhang is itself a valuation signal.
2. **PEG discipline with one sector exception.** Keep positions below **1.5–2.0x PEG** generally. The one sanctioned exception is FMCG/consumer, where PEG up to ~1.67x is acceptable because those earnings streams are more forecastable. A cited wealth-creation study found 3-year alpha over index of ~26% for PEG 0–0.5x, ~11% for PEG 0.5–1x, turning negative above PEG 3x — and buying anything above 50x P/E delivered roughly **-8% average 3-year alpha** historically. Treat ">50x P/E, no exceptional growth story" as a statistically poor entry point, not just a qualitative caution.
3. **Consistent-vs-volatile re-rating thesis.** A stock whose earnings profile transitions from *volatile to consistent* has historically been a bigger re-rating driver than growth or ROE improvement alone (a pattern a cited Motilal Oswal wealth-creation study attributes much of India's biggest multi-baggers to). Practical tell: management starting to use language like "sustainable momentum" in concalls, or several consecutive quarters of narrowing beat/miss variance, is the signal this transition may be underway — verify with the actual multi-quarter earnings variance, don't take the phrase alone as proof.
4. **Strip one-off items before computing any multiple.** Contract-termination fees, one-time tax credits, insurance settlements, and other non-recurring P&L lines can make a headline P/E look far cheaper than the underlying run-rate business — always recompute P/E on a "clean" PAT with disclosed one-offs removed, and flag the delta (a course example: a headline "23x" P/E became "45x" once a one-off ~₹500–600cr contract-termination gain was stripped out).
5. **Mid-CAPEX operating-leverage distortion.** A company mid-way through a large capex cycle shows an artificially high *trailing* P/E because new capacity hasn't yet contributed earnings (course example: a chemicals name at a headline "81x" trailing P/E while building a new plant). Fix: build a **forward P/E off management's own guided asset-turn/capacity-utilization ramp**, not trailing earnings — e.g., current EV ÷ 2-year-forward estimated PAT derived from a disclosed order book, used as a directional "attractive/unattractive" filter rather than a precise target.
6. **Low-float / cornered-stock distortion.** When promoter + DII + FII holding leaves a very small public float (course example: ~93% cornered float, <10% public float), the observed P/E can look artificially stable or elevated regardless of underlying fundamentals, simply because there isn't enough tradable float for price discovery — until an unlock event. Treat an unusually stable-looking multiple on a low-float name as a potential valuation trap, not confirmation of quality.
7. **The "double engine" of stock returns.** Total return = EPS growth × P/E re-rating, and this is **multiplicative, not additive** — 20% EPS growth combined with a P/E re-rating from 10x to 20x is a ~140% stock return, not 20%. The inverse ("reverse double engine") explains extended sideways/down markets where flat-to-declining P/E stacks on top of slowing EPS growth even as trailing earnings still look fine on paper — always check whether the *multiple* itself is a tailwind or headwind, separately from the earnings trajectory.
8. **Macro overlay: nominal GDP growth as the market-wide P/E driver.** Aggregate market P/E re-rates and de-rates largely with the trajectory of nominal GDP growth (real GDP + inflation) — a broad, multi-year market de-rating can be almost entirely a function of decelerating nominal growth, independent of any single company's fundamentals. A secondary sentiment gauge: **% of stocks trading above their 200-day moving average** — below ~20-25% signals high-fear/potential buying-zone conditions market-wide, above ~85-90% signals euphoria — read this alongside, not instead of, bottom-up valuation.
9. **Value-trap and growth-trap are symmetric risks.** Growth trap = high starting P/E (>30-35x) betting on growth that doesn't materialize (a cited multi-year example: heavy CDMO capex with flat PAT for 5-6 years with no clear resolution). Value trap = low starting P/E (<15x) with no earnings growth catalyst — a cited study found only a small fraction (roughly 1 in 50) of sub-15x-P/E stocks beat a broad index over the following 3 years. Rule of thumb worth encoding: **"value remains value indefinitely until a genuine catalyst or rate-of-change appears"** — a cheap multiple alone is not a thesis.
10. **Price-to-cash-flow as an operating-leverage/depreciation detector.** A large gap between a rich-looking P/E and a much cheaper price-to-cash-flow signals a depreciation-heavy capex phase or hidden operating leverage the P/E alone doesn't show — worth computing both whenever a name looks expensive on P/E but the business is capital-intensive. Rough ceiling flagged in the course: above ~22-23x price-to-cash-flow starts to look expensive on this lens too.
11. **Separate transactional P/E from intrinsic P/E.** Intrinsic P/E should move with genuine cash-flow/earnings prospects; transactional P/E moves with pure sentiment/news flow (a policy headline can move a stock's multiple same-day well before — or even without — any actual change to intrinsic economics). When a multiple re-rates sharply on a news day, ask which of the two just moved before updating a thesis.

## Samit Vartak's Framework — a named external perspective (Sageone Capital CIO)

**This section is explicitly Samit Vartak's own stated investing framework** (from a long-form interview, "Conversation with India's Super Investors"), kept distinct from SOIC's own teaching above because it is a specific, credible investment professional's personal methodology, not a house framework — cite it as "Samit Vartak's view" if referenced in output, not folded silently into generic guidance.

- **Value creation is not the same as multiple expansion.** Capital reinvested at a return above what the investor requires (their "expected return"/discount rate) creates real value (market value rises above book value); capital reinvested below that hurdle rate *destroys* value even while EPS keeps growing — a reinvestment-heavy company with sub-hurdle ROIC can see its fair P/E and P/B *fall* even as earnings rise (his worked arithmetic: 10% ROE reinvested at a 50% payout ratio can cut fair P/E from 6.7x to 5x and P/B from 0.67x to 0.5x).
- **The investor's own required/expected return is the single most sensitive lever on fair value** — more sensitive than growth rate or ROE. The same cash flows discounted at 12% vs 18% can differ in value by roughly 6x. This is why perceived-as-"safer," highly consistent businesses (his examples: Page Industries, Britannia, Asian Paints) sustain rich multiples even as growth slows — a lower-required-return investor base (large funds, FIIs) is willing to own them, which itself compresses the discount rate applied and expands the multiple. The multiple change here is about *who owns the stock and what they require*, not just the earnings outlook.
- **Duration of growth is a heavily underweighted valuation driver.** Comparing an identical ROE/growth-rate business with a 5-year visible growth runway against one with a 25-year runway can roughly double the justified fair P/E (his example: ~16.7x vs ~42x) — this is offered as the explanation for why certain "expensive-looking" compounders (Astral Poly, Bajaj Finance-style names, BKT) sustain premium multiples versus more "mature-looking" comparables with similar near-term growth.
- **True value investing is a risk-perception gap, not a low headline multiple.** A statistically cheap stock is not automatically a value opportunity if the achievable return is structurally capped (his example: a discount to land-bank NAV that only monetizes over 3 years is a mediocre return, not a bargain). Real value investing, in his framing, is finding a gap between the *market's* perceived risk of a business and your own better-researched view of that risk — when the market re-rates its risk perception downward (because your thesis proves out), the resulting flow of lower-required-return capital into the stock is what drives the re-rating, alongside earnings growth.
- **Distinguishing cyclical from structural growth is, by his own account, the single biggest source of investing mistakes** — including in his own history, across more than one cycle. Extrapolating a cyclical upcycle (a temporary anti-dumping duty, a short-lived supply-chain shift, a one-off demand spike) as if it were structural leads to painful de-ratings once the cycle turns. Every growth driver should be explicitly interrogated: is this durable, or a window that closes?
- **B2B businesses deserve structurally lower multiples than B2C for the same growth/ROE**, because customer concentration and switching dynamics create weaker moats than consumer brand loyalty — a cost-efficiency-based barrier to entry (typical of B2B) is less durable than a genuine consumer preference barrier.
- **For an individual investor with a long (7-10 year) horizon, earnings-compounding ability matters far more than precise valuation discipline** — his own framing: focusing purely on finding 25%-CAGR compounders and largely ignoring position-level valuation can itself deliver ~25% returns, because valuation mistakes wash out at the portfolio level over that horizon. This is explicitly a different risk/time-horizon trade-off than the position-level valuation discipline used elsewhere in this file — apply it as context for long-horizon individual portfolios, not as a license to ignore valuation in short/medium-horizon or fund-style work.
- **India-specific caution: promoter-cornered float can distort valuations upward artificially** — very low public float names can show high impact cost (his figures: 15-40%) even for exiting a small (~1%) stake, meaning the quoted multiple isn't a multiple any real capital pool could actually transact at scale. Treat such multiples skeptically rather than as genuine market-clearing prices (his examples: instances of float-driven distortion in names like HDFC and Havells Glasscoat-type situations).
- **Does not use DCF/terminal-value math in practice** — citing that even Buffett/Munger-style investors rarely visibly run one, because terminal-growth-rate assumptions are hyper-sensitive and unknowable even to the company's own promoter. Prefers directional judgment on ROIC-vs-expected-return and growth-duration over precision-looking DCF outputs. This is a useful counterweight to over-trusting a DCF's false precision elsewhere in this file's DCF section.
- **Governance/fraud detection is mostly qualitative** in his practice: industry scuttlebutt/network checks, comparing a company's disclosed per-unit capex cost against known industry cost benchmarks to spot possible siphoning, and checking court/high-court records tied to promoter names — a useful cross-check to run alongside the more quantitative red-flag checklists in `forensic-accounting`.

## Notable case-study takeaways (sector lessons embedded in named-company examples)

- **TCPL (Tata Consumer Products) — FMCG distribution-intensity discount.** A valuation gap vs peers (course-era: TCPL ~6x Price/Sales vs Britannia ~8x vs Nestlé >10x) traced directly to distribution intensity — TCPL's ~2.4mn outlet reach vs HUL's ~8-8.5mn — meaning the valuation discount reflected under-penetration, not a weaker brand. Distribution expansion (plus EBITDA margin expansion from cost rationalization, premiumization, and bolt-on M&A) was the explicit re-rating catalyst to track. General lesson for mature FMCG names: growth has to come from aggressive distribution expansion *or* cost rationalization — one lever has to be pulled hard.
- **Sudarshan Chemical — cyclical specialty-chemical scenario construction.** Bull/base/bear scenarios held revenue roughly flat and varied EBITDA margin instead (course-era: ~16% vs ~17.5-18% post-capex) — margin assumption, not revenue, was the swing variable. Applied an explicit peak-cycle-vs-base-cycle P/E split (a richer peak-cycle multiple reserved only for the bull case) — the general lesson: cyclical specialty/pigment-intermediate names need an explicit "which point in the cycle am I valuing this at" discipline, never a single static multiple.
- **Heranba — the agrochemical spot-selling discount**, folded into the Agrochemicals row above: near-identical gross margins to a regulated-market peer, yet a persistent valuation discount purely from unregulated/spot-market export exposure and a governance overhang (contingent liabilities at 61% of net worth in the course-era example) — a clean illustration that margin similarity alone doesn't equalize valuation across geography/contract-quality differences within the same sector.

## Sector → Valuation KPI Table

| Sector / Industry | Primary valuation metric(s) | Supporting KPIs to track | Benchmark / range notes (verify currency before citing) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Banks** | Price-to-Book (P/B) | ROE, ROA, GNPA/NNPA, credit cost (% of AUM), NIM, capital adequacy | Course-era anchors: large private banks ~2.8–3.4x P/B at ROE 14–17%/ROA 1.9–2.3%; a bank with rising ROE/ROA trend trading below peer P/B (e.g. Axis-style setup) flagged as a re-rating candidate — verify current multiples, these move every quarter |
| **NBFCs** | P/B, forward P/E | ROA (often structurally higher than banks), AUM growth, cost-to-income, credit cost, secured vs unsecured mix | Get higher multiples than banks for similar ROA (no capital-adequacy drag) but re-rate down faster in credit-cycle downturns — treat NBFC multiples as more volatile, less "sticky" than bank multiples |
| **Small Finance Banks** | P/B | ROA trajectory, secured vs unsecured mix, AUM growth | Wide P/B dispersion within the sub-sector even at converging ROA — a persistent gap vs a similar-ROA peer is itself a signal worth investigating, not necessarily a "buy the cheap one" trade |
| **Life Insurance** | Price/Embedded Value (P/EV) | VNB (Value of New Business) margin, persistency ratio, product mix (par/non-par/ULIP) | Cannot value off reported P&L — actuarial-assumption-driven; EV and VNB margin are the real earnings proxies |
| **Cement** | EV/ton (replacement-cost proxy) | Realization/bag, EBITDA/ton, total cost/ton, capacity utilization, top-N market-share concentration | Never use plain P/E — depreciation can be ~80% of EBITDA for some names. Pan-India players get materially higher EV/ton than regional players; check whether industry EBITDA/ton is at a cyclical low (new-entrant-driven price war) before treating a low multiple as cheap |
| **Real Estate** | Market Cap/Pre-sales; EV/Imputed(embedded) EBITDA | Pre-sales bookings trend, launch pipeline, geography/micro-market mix, imputed EBITDA margin (pre-sales × guided margin, NOT accounting P&L margin) | Course-era rule of thumb: >12–13x embedded EBITDA = good/premium name; <10x = cheap/under-recognized. Cannot value off P&L — 3-year project-delivery revenue-recognition lag means P&L losses can coexist with strong cash collection. Watch for the P&L inflection when a developer moves from "booking losses" to "revenue recognition" phase — looks like sudden margin expansion but is really an accounting catch-up |
| **Hospitals** (deep-dive) | EV/EBITDA, Price/Cash Flow; SOTP by geography/segment for multi-format chains | Bed additions, brownfield (2–3yr payback) vs greenfield (~6yr payback) capex mix, occupancy, **ARPOB** (avg. revenue per occupied bed — tier-1 city premium, e.g. course-era Max ~₹55k vs Apollo ~₹44k vs Fortis ~₹48k), EBITDA margin trajectory as new centers mature (12%→17-22%), % revenue from complex/high-margin therapies, international medical-tourism % of revenue (a recovery lever if depressed vs pre-COVID 10-12%), FCF = EBITDA − maintenance capex, net-debt-reduction trend | A market-cap/cash-flow multiple below ~25x flagged as a screening-level "worth a look" threshold. Bear/base/bull scenarios built on 15–25x EV/EBITDA; course-era caution that a hospital name at ~37x EV/EBITDA (Max, cited as an example) "has no margin of safety" and flagged sector-wide de-rating risk once every major chain is adding beds simultaneously (capital-cycle logic applies to hospitals too — watch industry-wide bed-capacity growth, not just the one company's). For multi-format chains with structurally different segment margins (e.g. domestic ~17-20% EBITDA margin vs an overseas/offshore segment at 35-40%), build **SOTP by segment** rather than one blended multiple |
| **Hotels** | EV/EBITDA, Price/Cash Flow | RevPAR (= Occupancy × ARR), occupancy %, ARR, room additions, segment (luxury/leisure vs business) | Once occupancy nears 85–90%, further RevPAR growth must come from ARR, not occupancy — check which lever is driving the number. "Good" hotel names: ROE/ROC 15–20%, RevPAR growth 10–15%/yr |
| **IT Services** | P/E, PEG, DCF | TCV (total contract value) / order-booking growth, revenue growth vs long-run mean, margin stability | Low depreciation makes plain P/E workable. Large-cap mature IT reverts toward mid-single-digit growth and a mid-20s-30s P/E; look for the fastest-growing sub-vertical inside a large-cap's business, then find smaller pure-plays in that same sub-vertical for a higher-growth/higher-multiple angle. Watch TCV re-inflection as the leading multiple-expansion trigger |
| **Pharma — CDMO** | P/E (pipeline-adjusted), Price/Sales | # commercial molecules vs pipeline stage (pre-registration/phase 1/2/3), client mix (domestic-contract vs MNC), capex tied to disclosed order wins | Hierarchy: domestic-contract-manufacturing (lowest multiple, capped margin) < MNC-contract-few-commercial-drugs (mid) < MNC-contract-large-pipeline (highest — optionality on TAM). SOTP may be needed if a company straddles CDMO + API/generic (weight each piece at its own multiple) |
| **Pharma — Generic/Formulations** | P/E, EV/EBITDA (cyclical) | US vs India export mix, branded vs unbranded % of India sales, FDA/plant-audit risk, distributor concentration | Hierarchy: US-generic-only-export (lowest, commodity pricing erosion, no moat) < mixed India+export < India-branded-only (highest, FMCG-like stable margin). Cannot be valued without first understanding where the sub-sector sits in its multi-year regulatory/pricing cycle — a rich multiple right before an FDA-audit-intensity or distributor-consolidation cycle turn is a trap |
| **Agrochemicals** | P/E, PEG | Patented vs generic molecule mix, MNC-parentage/insourcing risk, export vs domestic mix, monsoon dependency, **regulated (US/EU) vs unregulated/spot-market export mix**, registration-pipeline size in regulated markets, single-molecule sales concentration, contingent liabilities as % of net worth (governance) | Value-chain position drives the multiple far more than growth alone: MNC-tied/patented-molecule names get the highest multiples, pure domestic-generic the lowest, with partial-patented names in between. PEG as a sense-check across the tier. **Deeper nuance (Heranba case study):** spot-selling into unregulated export markets earns a "commodity" valuation discount *even at gross margins similar to a regulated-market peer* — geography and contract-stickiness are the re-rating lever, not margin. A pending regulated-market registration pipeline (e.g., targeting ~$50mn revenue in US/EU) can pre-rate a stock on the expectation of stickier future business before it shows up in the numbers. Single-molecule concentration above ~55% of sales is treated as a valuation-capping risk factor independent of near-term financials |
| **Commodities (steel, solar glass, base chemicals, paper)** | P/B, P/E, margins — but ALL secondary | Underlying commodity price trend (track via a live commodity-price source, not the stock's own chart), EBITDA/unit, capacity-expansion pipeline, anti-dumping/trade-policy timing | The company-specific multiple is close to meaningless without first pricing the commodity cycle — a "cheap" P/E during peak commodity prices is a value trap, not a bargain |
| **EPC / Infrastructure / Capital Goods** | Order book/Market cap, Order book/Sales, EV/EBITDA | Order-book quality (execution speed/short vs long-dated, geography, margin profile — NOT just size), government budget tailwind/headwind, backward-integration risk from customers | The market prices the forward order pipeline more than trailing earnings — a stock can fall on strong trailing PAT growth if the order book shrinks. Watch for order-book-size-driven multiple inflation on structurally low (<high-single-digit) EBITDA margins — that's a bubble pattern, not a quality premium |
| **Platform / Internet / Marketplace** | Price/FCF, P/E, P/CF-from-ops, DCF | Volume/transaction growth, paying-customer/subscriber net-adds, churn, revenue-per-user | The market prices net customer additions and churn ahead of trailing PAT — a name can hit new lows on churn deterioration even while PAT is still growing. Sanity-check any "normalized forward P/E" story against how far out the normalization year sits (a 100x+ multiple on a normalized year 3–4 years out is a red flag, not a discount) |
| **Capital Markets / Exchanges / Depositories** | P/E + position in the multiple cycle | Transaction-charge trend (best short-term leading indicator for the whole capital-markets complex), IPO-issuance-linked income | These names cycle hard with capital-market activity — a "cheap" trailing P/E right after a multi-quarter volume boom is likely mid-cycle, not a bottom; check where the current multiple sits vs its own historical cyclical range, not just vs today's absolute level |
| **Wealth Management vs. Broking** | P/E | AUA/AUM growth, revenue mix (transactional vs recurring/advisory), earnings cyclicality | Broking earnings are highly non-linear with trading volumes (steep drawdowns in downturns); wealth-management-mix names have lower earnings cyclicality (can sell debt/advisory products in downturns) and structurally deserve a steadier, sometimes higher, P/E than a pure broker — check the revenue mix shift, not just the label "broker" vs "wealth manager" |
| **Alcohol / Liquor** | P/E, Price/Sales | % of sales that is branded (vs bulk/ethanol/contract-manufacturing), state/geography presence, premiumization trend | 100%-branded distribution-focused names command the highest multiple; pure contract-manufacturing the lowest, with mixed models in between — track the branded-mix trend and new premium-brand launches as the re-rating trigger, not revenue growth alone |
| **FMCG / Consumer** | P/E, DCF | Volume growth (separate from price/mix-driven value growth), category penetration, category growth ceiling | The market punishes stalled volume growth hard regardless of "quality" or brand strength — even a defensive large-cap can see a multi-year-low P/E and a >30% drawdown on a volume-growth stall. Look for categories still growing in double digits (secular headroom) vs saturated ones (mature, low-growth ceiling). See also `_shared/sector-playbooks/fmcg-consumer-care.md` for the granular quarterly-tracking framework (RM basket, GST, seasonality) that sits underneath this valuation view |
| **Chemicals (specialty/commodity, B2B)** | P/E, Price/Cash Flow, EV/Cash Flow | Supply-side capacity utilization (demand-side matters less — largely a supply-driven commodity game except in branded/niche cases), margin trend over 8–10 years, capex/net-block growth plans | No brand power in most cases (a few niche exceptions) — apply the Over-earning check above rigorously; a rich multiple on a margin at a multi-year high is the single most common mistake in this sector |
| **Packaging** | Price/Sales, Price/Book | Value-added product mix (vs commodity film/laminate), customer concentration/proximity to end-customer plants | Stable-margin names in this sector typically serve FMCG or other proximate, less-competitive niches — check whether margin stability is structural (few competitors in that specific niche) or coincidental before paying up for it |
| **Auto Ancillaries / Export-linked Capital Goods** | P/E | End-user-industry demand cycle (this matters far more than the company's own standalone metrics), backward-integration/insourcing risk from OEM customers, customer/geography concentration | A low P/E tied to a structurally slowing end-market (e.g., a specific country's tractor/auto cycle) is a value trap, not a bargain — always identify and check the health of the end-market first |
| **Conglomerates** | Sum-of-the-Parts (SOTP) | Segment-wise EBIT/revenue mix, segment-appropriate multiple per business line (pull from this same table, per segment) | A blended single multiple across segments will misprice the business whenever one segment's economics dominate EBIT while another dominates the growth narrative (the "conglomerate discount" pattern) — always build SOTP with each segment's OWN sector row above, then sum |

## What could be wrong with this reference

- Every benchmark range above is a snapshot from a set of courses, recorded at various points across roughly 2021–2026, spanning different rate/growth-cycle regimes — India 10Y yields, Nifty aggregate P/E, and sector-specific supply-demand balances all move, and a range that was accurate when a given lesson was recorded can be stale, or even from a *different* point in the cycle than another lesson referenced right next to it in this file. Treat every range as a starting prior to be checked against current peer multiples, never as a standing truth — this matters more now that the sourcing spans multiple recording dates, not one single snapshot.
- The bulk of this file is instructional/retail-investor-oriented (SOIC), not a sell-side or academic source — company examples cited (Sun Pharma, HDFC Bank, ITC, TCPL, Sudarshan Chemical, Heranba, etc.) are illustrative teaching cases, not a systematic sample; don't over-generalize a single-company anecdote into a hard sector rule without checking 2–3 more names in that sector. The one exception is the §Samit Vartak's Framework section, sourced from a named, credible fund manager's own stated methodology in a long-form interview — still one person's framework, not a consensus view, and flagged as such throughout that section; don't blend his views into the SOIC-sourced material as if they were the same voice.
- Several sector rows (Packaging, Wealth Management, Auto Ancillaries) rest on a single or handful of named examples in the transcripts — thinner evidentiary basis than the Banks/Cement/Real Estate/Pharma/Hospitals rows, which had multiple worked examples and explicit numeric ranges across more than one lesson. Weight confidence accordingly when a skill cites this file.
- The §General valuation techniques section (items 1–11) mixes durable analytical discipline (stripping one-off items, checking float before trusting a multiple, separating transactional from intrinsic P/E) with specific cited statistics (the "-8% average 3-year alpha above 50x P/E" figure, the "~1 in 50 sub-15x-P/E stocks beat the index" figure) that were stated by the instructor as coming from named third-party studies (Motilal Oswal-style wealth-creation studies) but were not independently re-verified against the original study in this pass — treat the *techniques* as reliable teaching, and the *specific statistics* as claims to verify against the primary study before quoting in a published output.
- ASR (speech-to-text) transcription of spoken, sometimes Hindi-English-mixed lectures can introduce numeric errors (a "26x" could be a mis-transcribed spoken number) — any specific number pulled from here for a HIGH-STAKES output (e.g., a published target price) should be cross-checked against the original transcript's `transcriptTimestamped` field, or against an independent source, before being stated as fact rather than as framework. One lesson in this pass (Level 2 Hindi, "Case Studies: best way to teach valuations") was confirmed to be fully interpretable Hinglish with no meaningful untranslatable content, but future lessons pulled into this file may not be — flag explicitly if a future addition has any genuinely uninterpretable stretch.
